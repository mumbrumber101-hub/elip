## TREATISE I.

On the Principle of Individuation of Material Substance, and of Its Accidents.

That the principle of individuation of material things may be handed down completely, it has seemed good in this Treatise to discuss both the principle of individuation of material substance and that of its accidents.

## DISPUTATION I.

*On the principle of individuation of material substance, and of its accidents.*

Because material substance precedes its accidents in dignity, and its individuation is the root of the individuation of those same accidents, we therefore dispute in the first place concerning the principle of individuation of the aforesaid substance.

## DUBIUM I.

*Whether the substantial form is individual of itself, and the first principle of individuation and of the purely individual distinction of the whole composite?*

[To be noted. Nazarius. Gonzalez.] **1** For the understanding of the title it must be noted, with Nazarius, on the First Part, q. 3, a. 2, sole controversy; Gonzalez, q. 11, disp. 23, no. 12; and the common school of the Thomists, that we can speak of the principle of individuation of material substance in two ways. In one way, of the first total and adequate principle — namely, of all the things that concur essentially and *in recto* in the constitution of the material individual as such: namely, of this form and this union, and likewise of this matter and its union with this form; and hence, in the opinion of St. Thomas, of matter in some way signed by quantity (materia signata quantitate), through which matter itself is rendered *this*, as we shall see below. In the second way, of the radical principle, which is the first root to which the whole of individuation is ultimately reduced.

The title of this dubium, therefore, and likewise of the whole disputation, is not understood of that former principle, but of this latter principle of individuation and of the purely individual distinction whereby one composite is distinguished from another; because this principle, when compared with all the others, possesses with better right (potiori ratione) the status of being the first and radical principle, as is evident from the very terms, and will appear more fully from what is to be said below. We said, *of the purely individual distinction*, because in the present context we are not speaking of an individual distinction mingled with a specific distinction and in some manner taking its origin from it — the way in which this man, for example, and this horse are distinguished — but of an individual distinction having no specific distinction mingled with it, and thus remaining purely within the latitude of individual distinction.

Hence we are not speaking of the principle of individuation metaphysically taken. Both because it is certain that any material individual whatever is individuated metaphysically through its individual and metaphysical difference, which constitutes it under its species and distinguishes it from everything else that shares the aforesaid atomic species (species atoma). And also because it is sufficiently established that the difference, or individual principle taken in this way, must be drawn from some physical principle which is the first origin and first root of the metaphysical individual character (ratio), so that the aforesaid individuation is ultimately reduced to it: and thus it comes to the same thing to inquire after the first principle absolutely such, as to inquire after the first principle of material individuation considered in the physical order.

[Scotus. Fonseca.] **2** Wherefore neither Scotus, on 2 *Sentences*, dist. 3, q. 3, nor Fonseca, on 5 *Metaphysics*, chap. 6, q. 5, sect. 1, touched the point of difficulty which is controverted by the Doctors in this whole disputation, when, in deciding this disputation, they say only that haecceity (hæcceitas), or the metaphysical individual difference, is the principle of individuation. For, as we were just saying, the controversy and the point of difficulty concern only the first and fontal principle of individuation, into which the whole of it is resolved; and hence the first principle and physical root, as we were saying a moment ago.

We add, moreover, in the title that particle, *intrinsic*, in order thereby to intimate that in the present context no mention is being made of the first extrinsic principle — in the way that the efficient cause, and peculiarly God, is the first principle of the individuation of every creature — but of the first intrinsic principle of individuation of the whole substantial composite.

### § I. The first opinion is set forth.

[Nimphus. Perez. Zimara. Suarez. Averroes. Avicenna.] **3** The first opinion asserts that the substantial form is individual of itself, and the first, or chief, radical principle of individuation. So Nimphus, on 5 *Metaphysics*, disput. 5; Sebastian Perez, Bishop of Osma, on 3 *De anima*, conclusion 1; Zimara, theorem 97; Suarez, *Metaphysics*, disp. 5, sect. 4; for which are also cited Averroës, on 1 *De anima*, chap. 7, and on 3 *Physics*, comment 60, and book 4, comm. 38; Avicenna, 6 *Naturalium*, part 1.

[First argument.] It is proved, first. For the first and radical principle (of which we speak) of the individuation and purely individual distinction of the substantial composite must have two things: the first is, to be intrinsic to the composite itself; the second, to be maximally proper to it as it is *this* and individual: therefore this principle will be the substantial form, and not anything else. The antecedent is evident. For just as the first principle of specification of the same composite cannot but be intrinsic and maximally proper to it as constituted in its species, so the first principle of individuation of the aforesaid composite must intrinsically constitute it in the *esse* of an individual, and be exceedingly proper to it considered in the *esse* of such. The consequence, in turn, is proved and explained. Since the form has these two conditions, as will be evident to anyone who considers the matter, and nothing else besides it enjoys them: from the fact that neither can accidents be intrinsic to the aforesaid composite as it is *this* and individual — otherwise in the *esse* of such it would not be one per se, as will appear more fully both of itself and from what is to be said below — nor can matter, although it is intrinsic to the composite, be maximally proper to it: since the very same matter which now constitutes one substantial individual can straightway constitute another.

[It is confirmed first. St. Thomas. Complutenses.] **4** It is confirmed, first. For that is the first and radical principle of individuation of the substantial composite which is the first principle with respect to its *esse*; and for this reason St. Thomas, in the sole question *De anima*, a. 1, ad 2, said that each thing has *esse* and individuation according to the same: therefore the form will have the character (ratio) of the aforesaid principle. The consequence is evident. Because the form alone is the first and radical principle of existing, and therefore matter does not have proper existence of itself, but only by reason of the form, as our Complutenses teach in the book of the *Physics*, disp. 3, no. 70.

[It is confirmed second.] It is confirmed and explained, second. Because for something to be actually the first principle of individuation, and hence of individual distinction, supposes that it is first a being in act: therefore the character (ratio) of such a principle will belong to the substantial form alone. The antecedent is evident. Because just as being in any manner whatever the principle of something supposes the entity of the principle, so being first in act a principle supposes that it is a being first in act. The consequence, in turn, is proved. Because matter, so far as lies in itself, is only a being in potency, from the fact that its whole *esse* is to be pure potency; whereas the form alone is what is first a being in act, seeing that it alone is of itself and essentially act, just as matter alone is essentially pure potency.

[Second principal argument. Complutenses.] **5** Second, and principally. For the first and radical principle of specific unity is the form alone: therefore also of individuation. The antecedent is commonly received by the Philosophers and the Dialecticians, and is established from what our Complutenses assert in the *Logic*, disp. 5, no. 75. The consequence, in turn, is proved. For the reason why the specific unity of the substantial composite is taken from the form as from its first and radical principle is that, although it cannot be given without matter, nevertheless it presupposes matter as something potential and indifferent, while the form removes the aforesaid indifference and bestows the ultimate essential and specific complement. Therefore, since the same form bestows the ultimate substantial complement on the substantial composite as it is *this* and individual (and for this reason Aristotle, in 1 *De anima*, chap. 1, said that the form is what constitutes a "this something" [hoc aliquid]), and presupposes matter as something potential and indifferent, it will follow that it is also the first and radical principle of individuation.

[Third.] **6** Third. For this determinate form does not regard this determinate matter in such a way that it can, so far as lies in its own power, naturally be received in it alone: therefore it is not *this* and individual by reason of the matter, but by reason of itself: therefore it will itself be the first and radical principle of individuation of the whole composite. This last consequence is evident from the former. Since the only ground on which the character (ratio) of the aforesaid first and radical principle could be denied to the form would be that it was individual not by reason of itself but by reason of the matter, and hence that matter would have to be the first root of individuation. The first consequence, in turn, is established from the antecedent. For if the form were individual by reason of the matter and not by reason of itself, then the form would be *this* precisely because it demands to be received in this determinate matter and not in another: and hence, if the very same form can naturally be received in another, it is a sign that it is not *this* and individual by reason of the matter, but by reason of itself.

The antecedent, however, is proved. First, because the very same determinate matter which is under the form of the embryo is afterwards under the form of the animal — for example, of the horse: therefore the very same form which is now in this matter could also, so far as lies in the power of the form, be in another matter; because act and potency, according to what they have of themselves, must mutually correspond to each other. And also because, if this soul — an equine one, for example — were of itself apt to inform only this numerically same matter, then all the equine souls that could inform that numerically same matter at diverse times would have among themselves some real agreement which they would not have with the equine souls informing other matters: because all the former would have an aptitude for informing this numerically same matter, which the other equine souls could not inform; and thus beneath the specific concept there could be given a substantial objective concept common to many individuals of that species and not to others — which seems absurd.

[It is confirmed.] **7** It is confirmed. Because if the numerically same substantial form informs diverse matter at diverse times, then the composite resulting therefrom would be numerically the same as that which previously consisted of another matter and the same form; therefore it is a sign that the form is the first and chief principle of individuation of the whole composite, upon whose variation or non-variation the individuation of the aforesaid composite varies or remains the same. The consequence needs no proof. The antecedent, however, is proved. For the reason why, in the general resurrection, individual men will rise with their numerical unity is that, although it may by chance have happened that two men had the same matter, and this matter is bestowed on only one of them — as happens with those who are nourished on human flesh — nevertheless to each will be bestowed his own proper rational soul, and this soul will draw whatever matter it has to its own proper unity.

[Fourth. St. Thomas.] **8** Fourth. For if for any reason the form were not the first principle of the individuation and purely individual distinction of the whole composite, it would be above all because the form itself is limited by matter; but this reason is null: therefore, etc. The major seems perspicuous. Since, as the individual difference is of itself limitative and determinative of the specific character (ratio) proceeding from the form itself, it will very well be gathered that the aforesaid form is not the first principle of that difference, if it does not have limitation of itself, but is to be limited by matter as by a first principle. The minor, however, is proved. For otherwise neither could matter, and hence nothing else could, be the first principle of the aforesaid individuation. For just as matter limits the form, so the form limits matter, and so of the other things concurring in individuation, as is sufficiently evident both of itself and from what St. Thomas teaches in the First Part, q. 7.

[Last.] Last. Because at least the intellective soul is individual of its very self, and the first principle of individuation: and for this reason St. Thomas, 2 *Contra Gentes*, chap. 75, in the solution of the first argument, and chap. 81, says that just as the *esse* of the intellective soul is not caused per se by matter, so neither is its unity or individuation.

### § II. The opinion of Aristotle and of St. Thomas is more pleasing.

[Conclusion. It is proved first, from Aristotle. Nazarius. Ferrara.] **9** It must nevertheless be said that the substantial form is not individual of itself in such a way as to be the first and radical principle of the individuation and purely individual distinction both with respect to itself and with respect to the whole composite — and hence in such a way that the whole of the aforesaid individuation and distinction is ultimately reduced to it. So Aristotle, 5 *Metaphysics*, chap. 6, at the end, where he says that the distinction of individuals within the same species proceeds from the divisibility of matter. And in book 12, chap. 8, he asserts that the things that are many in number have matter; and from this he concludes that the first mover is one not only in notion (ratione) but also in number: because, since he is act, he does not have matter. But if the substantial form were individual of itself, in such a way as to have the character of the first and fontal principle of individuation and individual distinction both with respect to itself and with respect to the whole composite, then numerical distinction would proceed from it rather than from matter and its divisibility. And likewise Aristotle — speaking of physical matter, as is evident from the context, and as Nazarius, on the First Part, q. 50, a. 4, controversy 1, and Ferrara, on 2 *Contra Gentes*, chap. 93, in the second argument, well show — would not rightly infer that the first mover cannot be multiplied in number from the fact that he would not have physical matter, that is, prime matter, as is evident from the context: since, if once the substantial form is of itself one in number, in such a way as to be, both for itself and for the composite, the first ground (ratio) of unity and of purely numerical distinction, then with better right would the complete and per se subsistent form — such as the first mover is — have this with respect to itself.

[It is proved second, from Aristotle. St. Thomas.] The Philosopher asserts the same thing also in 10 *Metaphysics*, text 25, where, assigning the first and fontal principle of specific and individual unity and distinction, he says that the form is the first principle of specific unity and distinction, but matter of individual distinction; and thus he says: *Since the one is the ratio, and the other the matter: whatever contrarieties are in the ratio make a difference in species; but those which are in the concept together with matter do not.* These words St. Thomas explains in the same place, lect. 11, thus: *Because in the composite there are matter and form, the one is the ratio, that is, the form, which constitutes the species; the other is the matter, which is the principle of individuation: whatever contrarieties are in the ratio, that is, on the side of the form, make things differ according to species. But those which are on the side of the matter — which are proper to the individual, which is taken with matter — do not make things differ according to species.*

[It is proved finally from St. Thomas.] **10** And this same thing the holy Doctor also taught in 2 *Contra Gentes*, chap. 93, in the second argument, in these words: *Whatever things are the same in species but different in number have matter; for the difference that proceeds from the form induces diversity of species, but that which is from the matter induces diversity according to number.* He teaches the same also in the First Part, q. 50, a. 4; in *De ente et essentia*, chap. 6; and on book 2 [of the *Sentences*], dist. 3, q. 1, a. 4, where, in order to give the reason why the Angels cannot be distinguished by number alone, by the very fact that they are immaterial, he speaks thus: *For if they are held to be immaterial, then, since no form or nature multiplies number except in diversity of matter, it must be that a simple and immaterial form not received in any matter is one only. Hence whatever is outside it is of another nature, because it is distant from it according to form, not according to a material principle, of which there is none there.* And finally St. Thomas very often repeats this same doctrine, as will be evident from the places to be cited at no. 43. Hence both Aristotle and the Angelic Doctor are commonly followed by his disciples, and by many others, whom we shall cite at no. 44.

### § III. The opinion of Aristotle and St. Thomas is explained, and the first reason for it is discussed.

**11** But because the assertion needs some measure of explanation, it must therefore be briefly explained before it is proved. For in it that limiting particle was deliberately added, namely: *in such a way as to be the first and radical principle of the purely individual distinction both with respect to itself*, etc. — in order thereby to signify that in the assertion it is not denied that the form, of its very self and through its intrinsic entity, is proximately individual: since of its very self and through its intrinsic entity it has proximately and immediately to regard this matter and not that; and by reason of this habitude it is immediately and proximately singular. What we deny is rather this: that the aforesaid form has it from its very self, as from a first and radical principle, to be individual, or to individuate the composite; and accordingly this whole matter draws its origin from elsewhere, as from its first fount and root.

And although the assertion thus explained is proved with sufficient efficacy from that second reason (ratio) of St. Thomas which we shall adduce in the following dubium, at no. 52, to prove that matter signed by quantity is the first root of the individuation already spoken of, nevertheless in this and the following § we shall establish it by two other reasons.

[The conclusion is proved by the fundamental reason, from Aristotle and St. Thomas.] **12** Now the prior and fundamental reason (ratio) was adduced by the Philosopher and the Angelic Doctor in the places cited, and it can be set out in this form. The unity and distinction proceeding from the form, as from a first and radical principle, is specific unity and distinction: therefore it is not the purely individual unity and distinction of which alone we speak: and hence the form will not be the first and fontal principle of precisely individual unity and distinction. This second consequence follows openly from the prior; the prior, in turn, from the antecedent. The antecedent, moreover — besides being express in St. Thomas in the places cited — can be proved from this: that specific unity and distinction is formal unity and distinction, because it is a distinction according to essence, which is what is formal in each and every thing; whereas numerical unity and distinction is material unity and distinction. Therefore, since the distinction that proceeds from the form as from a first principle is formal and not material — inasmuch as the latter arises from matter as from its first root — it comes about thence that the unity and distinction proceeding from the form, as from a first root, is specific unity and distinction.

[An evasion from a twofold example.] **13** This reason is so efficacious that, once it is rightly weighed, all the evasions by which those adversaries meet it easily collapse. For in the first place some reply that not every unity and distinction proceeding from the form is formal. For just as two acts and forms, or two rational souls, can be distinguished as acts and forms, and nevertheless that distinction is not formal but material — and similarly two Angels, who are separate forms having nothing of matter, are distinguished individually — so some things can be distinguished in respect of the form, and by reason of it, and yet be distinguished not formally, but materially.

[It collapses.] This solution, then, if the reason given is rightly weighed, easily collapses. Since, when the matter is well inspected, it is repugnant that the unity and distinction proceeding from the form should not be formal, and hence specific. And this indeed can be declared and explained thus. The distinction proceeding from matter, insofar as it is matter, and as from a first and radical principle, cannot, insofar as it proceeds from matter, be formal or specific: therefore neither can the distinction proceeding from the form, insofar as it is form, as from a first root, be material; rather it must necessarily be formal, and consequently specific.

[St. Thomas.] **14** The consequence seems established by parity of reason (paritate rationis), and likewise by the example of the distinction proceeding from whiteness or from heat: which, because it proceeds from them as such, cannot but be in the character (ratio) of white or of hot, from the fact that these effects are the proper formal effects of whiteness or of heat — just as the proper formal effect of the substantial form is to bestow formal substantial *esse*. The antecedent is St. Thomas's, in the place cited from 10 *Metaphysics*, lect. 11, where he speaks thus: *Hence, if it be asked whether matter makes things diverse in species in some way — it seems that it does: because this horse is diverse in species from this man, and yet it is manifest that the ratio of each is with individual matter, and thus it seems that matter in some fashion makes things differ in species. But nevertheless it is manifest that this does not occur on account of the diversity of the matter, but because the contrariety is on the side of the form: thus, therefore, it appears that the contrariety which is on the side of the form itself makes things differ in species, but not that which is on the side of the matter.* And then the same antecedent can be urged. First, from the fact that an effect cannot be beyond its cause, and so formal unity and distinction cannot proceed, as from a first root, except from the form. And also from the fact that in material substances, although the generic character (ratio) could proceed from matter according to what the aforesaid character has of potentiality and materiality, it does not do so according to what it retains of actuality and formality — since according to this latter aspect it proceeds from the substantial form as such: therefore it is a sign that formal *esse*, or formal distinction, cannot proceed from matter as such.

[It is confirmed and explained.] **15** It is confirmed and explained. Since it is one thing that a distinction should be exercised in forms, and another that it should proceed from the forms themselves as such. Just as it is one thing that having entitative parts should be exercised and found in material substance, and another that having them should proceed from that same substance as such. Hence, just as it is not repugnant that the aforesaid parts should be found in, exercised in, and communicated to substance by another, as by their first root — namely, by quantity — and that nevertheless they should not be formally substantial (de formali), but quantitative, as our Complutenses teach at length and learnedly in the book *De Generatione*, disput. 5, no. 58 and following; whereas it would be otherwise if the aforesaid parts were exercised in substance and proceeded from substance as from a formal cause, for by that very fact they could not but be formally substantial: so — the due proportion being preserved — although it is not repugnant that a distinction exercised in substantial forms, yet proceeding from elsewhere, namely from matter, should be not formal but material, it would nevertheless be otherwise if it were exercised in the aforesaid forms and proceeded from them: since by that very fact such a distinction must be similar to its cause, and hence must be formal.

[An objection is solved.] **16** We said, *the due proportion being preserved*, so that a certain objection drawn from the example brought forward, which could here be raised, may stand solved. Because in the present matter we compare the thing with which we are dealing to the aforesaid example only in this: that just as the entitative parts of substance would have to be formally substantial (de formali) if they proceeded from substance and did not merely have to be exercised in substance — though it is otherwise if they are merely exercised in it — so the distinction proceeding from the form cannot but be formal, although the distinction exercised in it, and proceeding from elsewhere, namely from matter, can be not formal, but material.

[The reason for the disparity.] For the rest, with this there stands the fact that there is its own disparity between the example and the thing of which we speak, arising from this: that the aforesaid distinction exercised in forms proceeds from matter as from a terminus in some manner extrinsic — that is, not essentially constituting the form; and therefore the form is individuated, and distinguished proximately from another form, through its very self, that is, through the habitude which it bears to matter. But the entitative parts of substance proceed from quantity as from an intrinsic formal cause, in the way in which being white proceeds from whiteness; and thus substance does not have parts, nor their distinction, through its very self — that is, through a mere habitude to quantity as to an extrinsic formal cause — but precisely as it lies under quantity and insofar as it is informed by it.

[To the first example.] Nor does that first example, which is brought forward in this solution concerning the twofold rational soul, prove anything. Since two rational souls can differ materially, although they are acts and forms, precisely because they are individuated by matter, as by the first and radical principle of their individuation. Hence, although that distinction is exercised in substantial forms, it nevertheless does not proceed from them; and so it can be not formal and specific, but material and purely individual.

[To the second.] **17** Nor, again, does the second example — concerning the twofold Angel numerically distinct by reason of their individual differences — prove anything. First because, as we shall say below, the distinction that obtains between a twofold Angel by reason of their individual differences is not a purely individual distinction, arisen from purely individual differences: because in them specific and individual unity and distinction are not distinguished virtually, or with a foundation in reality (cum fundamento in re), but only on the side of our manner of conceiving, as we shall say, God granting, in the treatise on the Angels. And thus that very same (so to speak) individual distinction is a specific distinction, and hence can and must arise from the form insofar as it is form; whereas in the present matter we are speaking, as we have already touched upon, only of purely individual unity and distinction, which, by the very fact of being such, must be material and proceed from matter.

And also because, even granting gratis that in the Angels their individual difference is distinguished virtually from their specific difference, nevertheless this does not weaken the force of our reason (ratio), as will appear more fully from what we shall say below at nos. 56 and 57, where we shall elucidate this matter with testimonies of St. Thomas.

[Another evasion.] From this there will also collapse another solution applied by the adversaries to the reason of St. Thomas: namely, that the difference proceeding from the form is formal and specific when it arises from the form according to what is formal in it, but not when it proceeds from it according to what is material in it, although both are proper to such a form and belong to it as such. But since the individuation of the whole composite must proceed from the substantial form according to what is material in it, and not according to what is formal, therefore the distinction proceeding from the form according to this aspect (ratio) — namely, according to its individual difference, or haecceity — can be material and numerical, not formal and specific.

[It collapses.] From what has been said, then, this evasion collapses no less than the preceding one. That this may be better understood, there must be recalled to memory what we were saying at no. 1: namely, that the discourse is not about the individual difference, or the material aspect (ratio), or the principle of individuation metaphysically taken, but about individuation, or the material aspect and principle of individuation physically taken; since any metaphysical ratio whatever must be founded in a physical entity, physically taken, and be drawn from it as from a first root.

[The same evasion vanishes, and the reason of St. Thomas is weighed.] **18** From this, therefore, is drawn a fuller weighing of the reason (ratio) of St. Thomas, through which the aforesaid evasion vanishes. For either the substantial form, physically taken, and according as it is physically individual and the principle of individuation, bespeaks something physically material over and above itself as it is the first principle of specification and of specific distinction? or it adds nothing real, physically material, over and above itself as it is physically form and bespeaks physical formal *esse* as *quod*? If this second is said, then the form, according as it pertains to the physical order and has the character of a physical principle with respect to the composite, will be only the root of formal specific distinction: since in that order it adds nothing over and above itself that is physically material, but whatever is found in it is physically something formal, as *quod*. But if the first is asserted, then that material element, which is physically such, must arise, as from its first root, from matter; and hence, although it is exercised and found in the substantial form itself (of which alone we speak in this dubium), it must proceed not from it, but from matter: since, just as formal *esse*, physically such, cannot proceed from any other source than the form, so material *esse*, which physically has the character of such, cannot take its order from anything else, as from a first principle, but matter.

[It is confirmed and explained first. Complutenses.] **19** It is confirmed and explained from what we were just saying, weighed however in this form. By the very fact that in material substances there is found something material and metaphysical with respect to metaphysical composition — namely, the genus — this, precisely as it is something material, cannot but arise, as from its first root, from matter itself, and not from the form, as our Complutenses show from Aristotle and St. Thomas and by an efficacious reason in the *Logic*, disp. 5, no. 75 and following: therefore, by the very fact that in the substantial form, and likewise in the composite arising from it, there is found some other material aspect (ratio) in the metaphysical order — namely, the purely individual difference of such a form and composite from another of the same species — the aforesaid difference cannot but arise, as from a first principle, from matter, and not from the form itself. And so any difference arisen from the form as such, as from the first physical principle to which such an effect is ultimately reduced, must arise from that same form according as it physically bespeaks formal *esse* as *quo*; and consequently such a difference cannot but be at once formal and specific.

[It is confirmed second.] It is confirmed, finally. For although the material form has in itself material *esse*, in the manner touched upon by our Complutenses on 1 and 2 *Physics*, and matter likewise; nevertheless, when a comparison is made between the two, then the first fount (so to speak) of the aforesaid form's materiality is matter itself, and not the converse: therefore it is a sign that the whole material *esse* of the substantial form and of the composite, and hence the individual difference of both, must arise from matter as from its first root. The consequence seems legitimate. The antecedent, in turn, is proved. For this causal proposition is true: *Because the form is educed from matter, or because it depends according to its esse upon matter, therefore the aforesaid form is material;* but not the converse. For since material *esse* is potential *esse*, from the fact that it is opposed to actuality and formality, it must proceed, as from its first root, from what is first in this genus — namely, from the first and pure potency, that is, from matter — according to that Aristotelian axiom: what is first in each genus is the cause of the things that come after in that genus.

*[There follows § IV: The second reason for the Aristotelian and Thomistic doctrine is set forth — part b of this dubium.]*
