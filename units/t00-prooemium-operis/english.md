## THEOLOGICAL COURSE.

### Prooemium and Partition of the Whole Work.

[Num. 1.] Because a Prooemium and the division of the whole work are wont to conduce not a little to the understanding of it, we therefore deliver both in the first place.

### § I. Why this whole work is inscribed a *Theological Course*?

With good right is this work inscribed: *A Theological Course embracing the Theological Summa of the Angelic Doctor St. Thomas*. For just as a course of arts is named a *Course*, the metaphor being drawn from the act of running, whereby we press on swiftly, completing our journey in a short time; and is called *of Arts*, this name being taken in a broader signification, insofar as it embraces certain parts of philosophy — and therefore, that this nomenclature may not be dissonant from the thing that is inscribed by it, all the things that are discussed in it ought to be delivered briefly and succinctly, so far as can conveniently be done: so this whole work is inscribed a *Theological Course*. *Theological*, indeed, because treatises concerning God, that is, Theological treatises, are contained in it. But a *Course*, because the aforesaid treatises (though various and diverse, and otherwise diffuse enough, upon which more than twelve tomes might deservedly be consumed) — setting aside both many opinions of adversaries of little moment, and also not a few reasons of scant weight on behalf of the true opinion, which are not drawn from the inmost substance (visceribus) of St. Thomas — are distributed in this work, briefly and succinctly, so far as the matter that is treated and its due division allow, into six tomes, as that course of arts into four: so that thus this whole Theological journey, otherwise most long, and much longer, wider, and deeper than the journey of the arts, may be accomplished and run through in no long time; and so this whole work may deservedly be distinguished by the name of a Theological Course, just as that course of arts by the title of a course.

Hence, just as in a course of arts those disputations alone are raised which are commonly current in the schools, although Aristotle, their guide, touched upon many other things: so this Theological Course embracing the Theological Summa of the Angelic Doctor will satisfy its office if, out of the whole Theological Summa of the same St. Thomas, it shall have explained all those questions and articles which are commonly agitated in the schools and are wont to be dictated to students, and shall have raised the treatises and disputations that are desired for their more exact understanding, although the Prince of Theology, St. Thomas, adduced many other things in his Theological Summa. For just as in the aforesaid course of arts (that we may thus persist in the example adduced), once the more difficult matters have been discussed and explained, others are omitted — either because they are easy in themselves, or because, even though of themselves they introduce difficulty, they can nevertheless be learned easily from other more difficult matters already explained and received from the master, or by applying one's own and moderate labor: so in the Theological course, once all the more difficult questions of St. Thomas which are commonly current in the schools have been agitated and explained, then others will deliberately be omitted — either because they are easy in themselves, or because, though difficult in themselves, nevertheless, once all the others already mentioned have been discussed and called to examination, one can come to their understanding with easy effort, or by one's own and tempered labor, and without the help of a master.

### § II. This Theological Course and the course of arts of our Complutense College will be consonant with each other, and will mutually foster one another.

**2** For the rest, because the course of arts and the Theological course are so coordinated with each other that the former goes before and prepares the way for the Theological course, while the latter follows after and is, as it were, the complement and form of the other — the former being like the morning star (lucifer) announcing the latter, and the latter, because Theological, not because ours, being (so to speak) the Sun following after its morning star; yet in such wise that the one fosters the other, though on an unequal footing (dispari ratione), and both are necessary for forming, as it were, the skilled Theologian, the former after the fashion of a disposition, the latter in the manner of a quasi-form: therefore this Theological course, which the course of arts of the Complutense College [i.e., the Carmelite philosophy course of Alcalá, companion to this Salamanca theology course] of our Religious Order preceded, and for which it paved the way, will be so knit together with it, and they will so mutually foster one another, that they shall be altogether consonant and at peace between themselves; and the things that were explained and agitated in the course of arts — and those from which other Theological difficulties either remain altogether perspicuous, or are deduced with the easiest effort — will not again be called to examination in the Theological course. So too, the things pertaining to the course of arts which were there omitted for just causes, and which would greatly conduce to the Theological treatises, will be discussed in this Theological course.

### § III. Why in this work a beginning is not made from the explanation of the first and second question of the First Part of St. Thomas, but from the third.

**3** Hence, then, a sufficient rationale (ratio) of several things is given. First, indeed, why in this Theological course we leave the first and prooemial question of the Angelic Doctor, and also the second annexed to it, untouched and unexplained. For what is chiefly wont to be discussed in that first question is: what is the object of Theology? whether it is itself a science? and whether it is one according to species? whether it is one simple quality? whether it is subalternated to the science of the blessed? and what is required for this subalternation? and then how, with its subalternation to that science, and with the want of evidence of principles (in evidentia principiorum) which it has, it may stand that it is truly a science? and other things of this kind. But these things stand established with sufficient perspicuity from what our Complutense College has adduced, lucidly and at length, in its Logic, disputation 19, throughout the whole of it, and has applied in a special way to Sacred Theology, explaining how God is its object and how it has atomic unity (unitatem atomam). And therefore this Theological course will abstain from discussing these things again. So too, because the things that St. Thomas adduces in the second question are easy enough in themselves and in no need of exposition — especially presupposing what is handed down in the aforesaid disputation by our Complutense College, and what will be said by us in this first tome — we shall therefore institute no special disputation concerning it.

**4** Secondly, from what has been said the rationale is given why this Theological course takes its beginning from the explanation of question 3 of the Angelic Doctor — and this, indeed, on account of the two points already touched upon. First, because after the two prior questions, which for the reason stated we have left untouched, this third question follows in due order; and therefore, once those were set aside, the first place was owed to this one, which introduces a special difficulty and demands a peculiar treatise for its explanation. Next, because in it is touched that greatest controversy concerning the principle of individuation of material substance and of its accidents, and consequently concerning the principle of the purely individual distinction of any being whatsoever — a controversy which was deliberately omitted by our Complutense College for just causes, and to consult brevity; and which conduces greatly to many and most weighty difficulties, and is wont to be agitated in the schools. And therefore this course, which was obliged to foster that course of arts and to be fostered by it, could not but explain this third question of St. Thomas at length, as regards this point.

### § IV. Whence this whole work is to be divided, and what will be contained in its first and second tome?

**5** Thirdly, next, from what has been said it is deduced why and how this Theological course, to be made up of six tomes, ought to be divided and cut, as it were, into diverse members. For, as we were just now saying, its object is God. And because the parceling-out (participatio) of a science ought to be according to the partition of its object, or according to the diverse consideration of it, this course will therefore have to be cut according to the diverse aspects (rationes) that we can conceive in God. God, then, can be considered either according to himself, or as he bespeaks an order, or quasi-order, to creatures — in such wise, indeed, that the consideration of God according to himself precedes the consideration of him as he, as it were, regards creatures; and therefore in the first place in our Theological course we shall treat of God according to himself.

But because God according to himself can be taken in two ways, either as he is One, or as Trine; and the things that regard God as One are prior to those that pertain to him as Trine: therefore discourse will be instituted first concerning God as One, before God as Trine. And so we shall give this first tome of our Theological course to God as One, and in it we shall set forth five treatises. And in the first, the disputation will be on the Principle of individuation of material substance, and of the purely individual distinction of any being whatsoever, in connection with the third question of this First Part of St. Thomas, so that thereby the divine unity, simplicity, and spirituality may be established. For, in order to explain all these things, St. Thomas sets it before our eyes as if by antithesis, explaining how spiritual substance, and above all divine substance, is distinguished in this from material substance, that the latter is individuated by matter, as by its first root, while the former is individuated of itself; and the reasons which press toward showing the material mode of individuation, drawn from matter, prove convincingly that immaterial substance, and above all God, is to be individuated in the opposite and more spiritual mode. In the second, of the visibility of God as he is in himself by the created intellect — that is, of the vision of God — together with the Angelic Doctor, q. 12. In the third, of the knowledge of God, in connection with q. 14 of the most holy Teacher. In the fourth, of the will of God, explaining question 19 of the same St. Thomas. In the fifth, finally, discourse will be woven concerning predestination, together with the Angelic Doctor, q. 23 of his First Part. Nor will the other divine attributes be treated of in particular in this first tome: both because this is not wont, by common usage, to be discussed in the schools in particular and dictated to students; and also because whatever shall introduce difficulty concerning the other attributes will be established entirely from what we shall say concerning these attributes of God already intimated.

**6** But because, as we were saying, in the consideration of God according to himself, immediately after God as One there follows God himself as Trine, for that cause the second tome of our Theological course will institute its discourse concerning God as Trine, together with St. Thomas, from q. 27 to q. 43 of his First Part. Nor will it halt there, but will proceed, together with the most holy Teacher, to the treatise on the Angels, from q. 50 of the same First Part to q. 64, and from q. 106 to q. 108.

But the reason (ratio) why, after the treatise on God as Trine, we shall add this treatise on the Angels, is that, as we asserted above, after the concept of God according to himself, as One and Trine, one comes in due order to God as he bespeaks a quasi-order to creatures. And because this quasi-order is twofold, either of efficient or of final cause, and the order, or quasi-order, of efficient cause is prior as regards the *esse* of existence simply such — namely, as regards the *esse* of natural, and not merely intentional, existence — therefore God is treated first as he is the efficient cause of creatures.

And although God can be considered either according as he is the cause of creatures, without determining in particular of which he has been the cause, or according as he is the cause of creatures in special — namely, of those which he has in fact produced: yet because that former consideration is neither wont to be commonly agitated in the schools, nor, presupposing what our Complutenses have said in the books of the *Physics*, and the other things that we shall assert concerning God as he is the cause of creatures in particular, does it need new exposition: therefore, although St. Thomas treated of this before the questions on the Angels, we nevertheless shall not raise special disputations about this matter in our second tome, but shall treat only of God as he is the efficient cause of creatures in particular, namely of the Angels, and hence of the Angels themselves proceeding from him. And although concerning man also, and his soul, many disputations could be moved, and a special treatise instituted concerning him, in order to explain the mind of St. Thomas in this First Part, from q. 75 to q. 90; yet because our Complutense College has handed down many things on this point, lucidly enough, in its course of Arts, in the books *On the Soul*: therefore nothing will be said of this *ex professo* in our Theological course.

### § V. What will be delivered in the third and fourth tome.

**7** Next, we shall give the third tome to the consideration of God as he is the ultimate end of the rational creature and its beatitude; and also to human acts, whether good, or evil and sinful — considered, however, in common — by which one arrives at God the ultimate end, or withdraws from him; and finally to the supernatural principle of good acts, whereby we merit eternal life, namely grace. And so this tome will embrace: first, the treatise on the ultimate end and beatitude, in connection with the things that St. Thomas discusses from q. 1 of his I-II to q. 5 — although we shall linger but little on these, because very many things contained there will be established from what we shall say in this first tome, in the treatise on the vision of God; then also the treatise on human acts in common, in connection with certain questions in which the Angelic Doctor treats of them in the same I-II; then, next, the treatise on sins, embracing the things that the most holy Teacher disputes in the same place, from q. 73 to q. 89; then, finally, the treatise on grace in common, on sanctifying grace, or on justification and merit — containing, therefore, and explaining what the Angelic Doctor adduces from q. 109 to the last question of his I-II, namely q. 114. Although perhaps we shall also add, before the treatise on grace, another on laws.

**8** But because, after the consideration in common of the things by which one arrives at God the ultimate end, or withdraws from him, there remains the consideration in particular of the things by which this same thing is done: therefore the fourth tome of our Theological course will dispute of this *ex professo*. And although, for this to be performed exactly, it would be necessary to treat of all the virtues and vices in particular; nevertheless in this fourth tome discourse will be woven only concerning the three Theological virtues, namely faith, hope, and charity: because it is concerning these alone that treatises and disputations are commonly wont to be agitated in the schools and dictated to students. Chiefly because, presupposing what will be said in the third tome concerning the I-II of St. Thomas, and what will be brought to light in particular concerning these three Theological virtues in this fourth tome, knowledge of the rest will easily be had, and without the help of a master. And therefore this fourth tome of our Theological course will embrace and explain the things that St. Thomas hands down concerning these virtues in his II-II, and which are commonly wont to be taught and dictated in the schools.

### § VI. What will be discussed in the fifth and sixth tome.

**9** For the rest, because this way of the virtues — by which one goes by a straight path to the heavenly fatherland, and from which we have departed by sinning — our Redeemer Christ, who came to save sinners, and who was given as Leader and Teacher of the nations, has shown to us in his own person; and because through him all benefits and gifts of grace are derived from God upon us: therefore, for the consummation of the whole Summa of Theology, it was fitting that the Angelic Doctor should treat thoroughly of this our Savior and of his peculiar benefits. And therefore we, together with him, shall give the fifth tome of our Theological course to this so great author of our salvation, and in it, through diverse treatises, we shall explain the whole matter of the Incarnation, from q. 1 of the Third Part of St. Thomas to q. 26.

**10** But because the special organs by which Christ the Lord communicates his grace to us are the sacraments, therefore St. Thomas treats of these in general from q. 60 to q. 65 of his Third Part, and afterwards of Baptism, from q. 66 to q. 71; of Confirmation, in q. 72; and of the Eucharist, from q. 73 to q. 83. The questions on Penance he began at q. 84 and continued to q. 90, which is on the parts of Penance, although, forestalled by death, he could not complete them; but the questions that were lacking up to the consummation of the work were added by his disciples from his other writings — which for this cause are named the Additions to the Third Part. And in them, from q. 1 to q. 28, are delivered the things that were desired for a complete treatise on Penance. Then from q. 29 to q. 33 Extreme Unction is treated, and Order from q. 34 to q. 40; and finally Matrimony, from q. 41 to q. 68.

Hence the sixth tome of this our Theological course will be wholly given to the explanation of the Sacraments, through which, as through certain heavenly organs and instruments, divine grace is derived from Christ upon us. Not for that reason, however, shall we treat of all the Sacraments in this last tome, but only of the Sacraments in general, and of the Sacrament of the Eucharist and of Penance: since Baptism, Confirmation, Order, and Extreme Unction are not commonly wont to be discussed in the schools, nor are treatises on these Sacraments wont to be dictated to students by their masters. So neither is a treatise on Matrimony commonly wont to be instituted among scholastic Theologians, although among moral Theologians no small disputations are raised concerning it. But we intend to deliver a course of scholastic rather than of moral Theology; and therefore we shall treat severally, in this our Theological course, of the Eucharist and of Penance, in which very many scholastic questions are involved. Wherefore it is no wonder that we weave no disputations concerning Matrimony, in which few or no things are contained that introduce new difficulty and regard scholastic Theology — chiefly because, as we have often said, once the other treatises already mentioned, which are contained in this whole Theological course, have been delivered, all the things that regard moral Theology will easily be learned, and dug out from St. Thomas.

**11** From all this it also comes about that, once this last tome has been brought to light by us, no other will be desired for the things that are delivered in the Third Part of St. Thomas after the questions on the Sacraments. For although the most holy Teacher, from q. 69 to q. 99 — which is the last of the Additions, and in which all the questions of his whole Theological Summa are brought to a close — treated of the things pertaining to the resurrection of bodies and the final state, and also of death, which precedes the resurrection, and of the final judgment, which follows it, and of the rewards and punishments of those who rise again; nevertheless, because some of these things will be established from what will be said in this first tome, in the treatise on the vision of God, and in the third tome, in the treatise on beatitude, and the others are not wont to be summoned to the scholastic schools: therefore we shall abstain from making discourse of them, and in this sixth tome our whole Theological course will be brought to an end.

### § VII. The guides of this our course are assigned; and at the same time it is further explained why it will not be at variance with the course of arts of our Complutense College.

**12** In it, however, as we have already intimated, we shall always keep two things before our eyes. The first, indeed, that the voice of the holy Fathers, but chiefly of Augustine and St. Thomas, may ever sound and resound in it; that it may ever pour forth their flowering and abundant doctrine; that it may ever drink from their most limpid and most sweet fountain, and pour back the waters once drunk from it. For no one will be able to cross this deepest ocean of Theology except with these very guides. The second, that all the things that will be discussed in this Theological course may ever be highly consonant with what the Complutense College of our sacred Order has lately published in its course of arts. For since both this course and that have been drawn from the inmost substance (visceribus) of St. Thomas, and the inmost substance of the Angelic Doctor does not contradict itself, but coheres with itself in a marvelous bond; neither will the course of arts and this Theological one contradict each other, but with a friendly covenant, and with the kiss of inmost peace, they will kiss one another.
