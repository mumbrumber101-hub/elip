## DUBIUM II.

*Whether matter according to itself, and precisely taken, or rather matter signed by quantity, is the first radical principle of individuation of the whole substantial composite?*

**33** From what has been said in the preceding dubium it is sufficiently established that matter is the first and radical principle of individuation of the whole composite, and hence also of the substantial form. But once this has been explained, it at once strikes the mind to inquire whether matter according to itself, and precisely taken, or rather as signed by quantity, has the character (ratio) of the aforesaid first and radical principle. And once this has been explained, this whole matter will remain elucidated. For although the union of matter and form also concurs *in recto* toward the adequate individuation of the composite, nevertheless it is certain that the individuation — both of the whole itself and of the matter or the form — cannot ultimately be resolved into that union as into a first principle: since the singular and individual union, as it holds itself on the side of the matter, must certainly presuppose that matter individual. And similarly the individual union of the form cannot but suppose the same form — and hence also the matter — as singular and individual: since such a union is *this* one and not *that* one precisely because it is of this form; and it is of this form because this form is of this matter, as is established from what has been said; and on this account we raise no peculiar dubium on this point concerning the union. But because the title of the dubium carries some ambiguity on its face, it is necessary to explain it before we propose the opinion of our adversaries.

### § I. Explication of the title.

[Observation.] **34** For the explication of the title, then, it must be noted that by the name of matter signed by quantity three things can be understood. The first, indeed, is a certain accidental composite arising from matter and from quantity itself actually and in exercise possessed. The second is matter itself — not as it expresses quantity as its formal element (de formali) and *in recto*, but as it expresses a certain mode superadded to the same matter, by reason of which it is determined, or signed, by a certain quantity, that is, by this quantity and not by that. And so it imports a certain concrete, or quasi-concrete, arising from the aforesaid mode, or sealing (sigillatio), and from matter itself, by reason of which it is said to be signed by the aforesaid quantity. Whence, although this mode, and the determination or signation caused through it, and consequently the matter as thus con-signed, is imported *in recto*, nevertheless it is by means of that mode that matter is said to be signed by quantity, because quantity itself is imported *in obliquo*; nor, without quantity thus imported, can matter be rendered signed unto a certain and determinate substantial form, nor unto a certain and determinate quantity. And so this whole matter is best explained by this, that matter signed by quantity is the first principle of individuation, since in this complex both the matter and the mode and signation are placed *in recto*, while the quantity is placed *in obliquo*.

The third is matter itself, as it expresses the pure and bare entity of matter without the aforesaid mode superadded to it and distinct from it *ex natura rei*, yet with a certain connotation — that is, insofar as it expresses matter itself as pre-possessing, after the manner of a first root, this quantity and not that; or (as many will have it) insofar as, after the manner of a first and radical principle, it expresses a connection, or habitude, to this quantity and not to that: for by reason of this it is designated and determined to this form and this composite rather than to that.

**35** The title of the dubium, then, is not understood of matter signed by quantity insofar as it peculiarly and determinately expresses some one of the signations just recounted, but as it abstracts from all of them and expresses matter signed, or determined, by quantity, whether this determination come about in this way or in that from among those just stated. We shall explain, however, in the following dubia, by which of them it must be signed in order to be the first and radical principle of individuation, so that we may thus separate the more certain and more received points from those not so certain and received. For that matter signed by quantity is the principle of individuation is the more certain point, and commonly received among Thomists; although it is not so certain and received by which of these aforesaid ways it is signed, or determined, by quantity, in order to be the first radical principle of individuation.

From this it is also understood why St. Thomas and his disciples use this complex, namely *matter signed by quantity*, to inquire whether matter, as by some one of the ways stated it brings quantity, has the character (ratio) of the first and radical principle of individuation. Because, evidently, as we shall say below and have just now touched upon, it is proper to matter, as by some one of the ways stated it imports quantity, to be rendered appropriated, determined, and signed unto having this form rather than that, and unto being a part of this composite rather than of that, and unto being the root both of this form and of this composite, as they are *these* and individual.

**36** And this can be explained further: from the fact that it is the same to inquire whether matter signed by quantity is the first root of individuation, as to inquire whether matter, as it is *this* and, in the manner due to it, is altogether singular by means of quantity, is the principle of the aforesaid individuation; because it is the same for matter to be altogether singular as for matter to be signed: since only singular matter can, so far as lies in itself and setting anything else aside, be designated; and on that account the Dialecticians, to signify a singular, or an individual altogether determined, use the name of the signed individual (individuum signatum). Whence — because, if matter, as by some one of the ways stated it brings quantity, can be the principle of individuation, it must be so for this reason, that, as it imports quantity, it becomes *this* and altogether singular, and hence becomes signed matter — therefore, to inquire whether matter, as in some way from among those explained it brings, or involves, quantity, is the first root and principle of individuation, St. Thomas and his disciples most fittingly use this manner of inquiring, namely whether matter signed by quantity — or (what is the same) matter made, in the manner due to it, *this*, determinate, and singular, and designated by means of quantity — is the first principle of the aforesaid individuation.

### § II. The first opinion is adduced.

[Albertus.] [Suarez.] **37** The first opinion asserts that matter according to itself, precisely taken — not, however, as sealed by quantity — is the first and radical principle of individuation of the whole substantial composite. So Albertus, as reported by Flandria, on Metaphysics VII, q. 22, a. 1, with whom Suarez and others agree in part, as is established from what was said in the preceding dubium.

[It is proved first, from St. Thomas.] It is proved first. For St. Thomas affirms absolutely that matter is the first principle of individuation of the substantial composite, no mention being made of quantity, or of signation by means of it: I, q. 3, a. 2 ad 3, and in many other places gathered in the *Tabula aurea*, under the word *individuum*, no. 21, and peculiarly in the opusculum De principio individuationis, in these words: *That, therefore, which is the first subject of all things in the way of generation, and incomplete, which is predicated of nothing of that genus — namely matter — will necessarily be the first principle of incommunicable esse, which is proper to the individual.*

[It is confirmed first from the same.] It is confirmed first. For in the same opusculum he not only said absolutely that matter is the first principle of individuation, but added that to it alone does the character of such a first principle belong; and thus he says: *For matter alone is the principle of individuation, as regards that in which the character (ratio) of first is preserved.*

[It is confirmed secondly from the same.] It is confirmed secondly from the same holy Doctor, I, q. 29, a. 1, where he establishes the difference between the individuation of accidents and that of substance — that substance is individuated through itself, but not so accidents; and thus he says: *Although the universal and the particular are found in all the genera, nevertheless the individual is found in a certain special way in the genus of substance: for substance is individuated through itself, but accidents are individuated through their subject, which is substance: for it is called this whiteness insofar as it is in this subject. Whence also the individuals of substance fittingly have a certain special name before others.* Which he also taught in q. 9 De potentia, a. 1 ad 8, saying: *Accidents are not individuated except from their subjects: but substance alone is individuated through itself and through its proper principles; and therefore it is fitting that in the genus of substance alone the particular has its proper name.* Wherefore, if once matter is the first principle of individuation of the whole material substance, as we said in the preceding dubium, it will not be able to have the character of such a principle as it imports accidents, but according to itself, and precisely taken.

[It is proved secondly by reason.] [Complut.] **38** Secondly it is proved by reason. Since matter according to itself, and precisely taken, is the first part and the first root of the substantial composite: therefore in the same way it will be the first part, and the first root and principle, of its individuation. The antecedent seems perspicuous. Since, as our Complutenses teach on the books of the Physics, disp. 15, no. 19 and following, when a comparison is made between matter and form, which alone are the parts of the substantial composite, matter is prior to form, from the fact that the latter is educed from the potency of the former. The consequence, moreover, is proved. Since, inasmuch as each thing is in itself one and individual just as it is a being, it thence comes about that what, taken by itself with precision, is the first part and first root of the substantial composite, is also the first part and first root of its individual unity, and hence of its individuation.

[It is proved thirdly.] Thirdly. For just as specification is taken from the form, so individuation from the matter, as is established from what was said in the preceding dubium: therefore the individuation of the substantial composite will be taken, as from its first root, from matter according to itself, and not as it in some way brings quantity, or as signed by quantity. The consequence is evident. Because specification, by the very fact that it is taken from the form as from its first root, is taken from the form alone according to the things which it brings of itself and by its own merits precisely regarded: therefore, by the very fact that individuation is taken from matter as its first root, it must be drawn from matter according to itself, and according to the things which it brings precisely from its own merits, and not as it in some way imports quantity.

[Fourthly.] [Complut.] **39** Fourthly. For generic unity is taken, as from its first root, from matter alone, according to what our Complutenses excellently teach in the Logic, disp. 5, no. 75: therefore so too individuation, or individual unity, by reason of which one individual differs in number alone from another. The consequence is evident from parity of reason (ratio). For generic unity is taken from matter alone, as from its first root, precisely because such unity is material — from the fact that it is proper to the generic ratio, which stands after the manner of matter and potency with respect to the specific differences: therefore, since individual unity is also material, it will follow that it too is taken from matter alone. Above all because individual unity is more material than generic unity: since the genus is numbered among the formal grades with respect to the species, although it stands after the manner of matter with respect to the specific difference; whereas individuation, and individual unity, stands only after the manner of a condition, or material determination, with respect to the species.

[It is confirmed.] It is confirmed. For matter of itself is incommunicable to anything in the way in which a superior ratio is in its inferiors, because of itself it is irreceivable in anything else: therefore of itself, and precisely from its own merits, it will be the first root of individuation. The consequence is evident. Since what is chief in the individual as such is the character (ratio) of the complete incommunicability just mentioned: and so that will suffice for it, after the manner of a first root, which from its own merits is, in the way intimated, incommunicable.

[Fifthly.] **40** Fifthly. For prime matter — before it actually receives in itself this quantity rather than another, or is determined to it through some mode distinct from it *ex natura rei*, or pre-possesses it in itself radically, or expresses a habitude or connection with it — must be pre-understood as singular and individual: therefore matter itself according to itself, and taken with precision, will be individual, and the first root of the individuation of the whole composite. The antecedent seems to stand of itself. Since this quantity rather than that — or (what is the same) singular and individual quantity — cannot be received except in singular matter. Just as neither can common matter, but only singular matter, be determined to this quantity rather than to that, or express a habitude to it, or contain it after the manner of a root: since common matter is of itself indifferent to any quantity whatsoever.

The consequence, as to its first part, is established from the antecedent. For by the very fact that matter is presupposed individual before it in any way imports singular quantity, it cannot on any ground be rendered singular through that quantity — just as neither through this substantial form rather than that, nor through any other singular accident whatsoever: since the substantial form also, and all singular accidents, are received in singular matter, and hence presuppose it singularized: therefore it will be necessary that matter be of itself individual.

As to its second part, the same consequence is likewise established. Since matter is the first part of the substantial composite: and so, by the very fact that it is of itself, and not as signed by quantity, individual, it cannot but be the first root of the individuation of the composite: just as by reason of itself it is the first root of its materiality.

[Lastly.] **41** Lastly. Since matter cannot be the first root of the individuation of the composite insofar as it is actually affected by quantity and includes it, nor insofar as through some superadded mode, distinct from it *ex natura rei*, it is determined to this quantity rather than to that, nor, finally, as it in some way pre-possesses this numerically one quantity: therefore in no way will it be able to be the principle of individuation as it is sealed by quantity. The consequence needs no proof, supposing the things we said in the explication of the title: since, as is established of itself and from what was said there, matter can be signed by quantity only in these three ways, and, thus signed, be the first root of individuation.

The antecedent, as to its first part, seems certain. Since no accident can be required for the individuation of the substantial composite as something actually and in exercise possessed, or intrinsically included in the individual itself, or in the principle of individuation: and so, if God were to separate quantity and the other accidents from Peter, he would nevertheless remain with his individuation. Just as, although in the Sacrament of the Eucharist he separates the accidents of the bread from the bread itself, nevertheless those accidents remain individual and singular. And this must be said with better reason of the substance itself separated from the same accidents: since it depends on them less than the accidents depend on it.

[Complut.] As to the second part, it is also clear. Since beyond matter, form, and union, no other entity or substantial mode is found in the substantial composite, as will be more fully established from what is to be said in dubium 4. As to the third part, the same antecedent is likewise established. Since this numerically one substantial form is introduced into matter before this numerically one quantity: for, as is evident from what our Complutenses teach in the books De Generatione, disp. 2, nos. 68 and 76, quantity is not received in the matter alone, but in the whole composite: therefore matter will contain, after the manner of a root, this substantial form before this quantity: and hence, since matter, as in that prior [moment] pre-possessing this numerically one form, cannot but be singular in the same prior, it will be unable to be individuated through its pre-possessing this numerically one quantity rather than that.

[It is confirmed.] [Aristotle.] [St. Thomas.] **42** It is confirmed. For if matter as signed by quantity is the principle of individuation, it would thence follow that both matter itself and the form — as in very deed and in execution it bestows the first grade, namely substantial *esse* — are not something singular, but only something common: but this is absurd: therefore, etc. The minor is established of itself: since whatever is given actually and in exercise on the side of the thing must be something singularized. The inference (sequela), however, is proved. For, as St. Thomas teaches from Aristotle, I, q. 76, a. 6, the form is immediately united to matter as regards the first grade which it confers upon it, in such wise that for this quantity is not required even after the manner of a disposition, or in the genus of material cause — from the fact that matter of itself is determined to receiving the first grade that can come to it from the substantial form, namely substantial *esse*: therefore, if matter is not individual except as sealed by quantity, it will be unable, in that prior in which in very deed and in execution it receives the form according to the said grade, to be singular: and hence neither will the form, which bestows that grade in the same prior, be singular.

### § III. The true and ancient opinion of Aristotle and St. Thomas.

[Conclusion.] [It is proved from Aristotle.] [St. Thomas.] **43** It must nevertheless be said that matter sealed by quantity in some one of those three ways recounted in the explication of the title from no. 34 up to no. 37 is the first and radical principle of the individuation of the whole substantial composite, and hence also of the substantial form itself. So Aristotle, Metaphysics V, text 12, in these words: *Further, some things are one according to number, others according to species, others according to genus, others according to analogy: in number, indeed, those whose matter is one*, etc. Which words St. Thomas explains thus, ibid., lect. 8: *He sets down another division of the one: that some things are one in number, some in species, some in genus, some by analogy. Those things are one in number whose matter is one. For matter, according as it stands under signed dimensions, is the principle of individuation of the form; and on this account the singular has it from matter that it is one in number, divided from others.* And in the same way the Angelic Doctor expounds this same testimony of the Philosopher on Boethius's De Trinitate, q. 4, a. 2 ad 1, in these words: *When the Philosopher says that those things are one in number whose matter is one, it is to be understood of signed matter, which stands under dimensions; otherwise one would have to say that all generable and corruptible things are one in number, since their matter is one.*

[It is proved from St. Thomas.] The Angelic Doctor teaches the same also in De ente et essentia, chap. 2, letter H, where he speaks thus: *And therefore it must be known that matter taken in just any way is not the principle of individuation, but only signed matter. And I call signed matter that which is considered under certain dimensions. Now this matter is not placed in the definition of man as man, but it would be placed in the definition of Socrates, if Socrates had a definition; in the definition of man, however, is placed unsigned matter. For in the definition of man is not placed this bone and this flesh, but bone and flesh absolutely, which are the unsigned matter of man.* And in the opusculum cited on Boethius, q. 4, a. 2, in the solution ad 2: *Since dimensions are accidents per se, they cannot be the principle of the unity of an individual substance; but matter, as it stands under such dimensions, is understood to be the principle of such unity and multitude.* And in the solution ad 4 he asserts thus: *Those things which differ in number in the genus of substance differ not only by accidents, but also by form and matter: but if it be asked why this form differs from that, there will be no other reason (ratio) except that it is in this signed matter; nor is any other reason found why this matter is divided from that, except quantity: and therefore matter subject to dimension is understood to be the principle of this diversity.*

[St. Thomas.] [Capreol.] [Cajetan.] [Soncin.] [Ferrara.] [Flandria.] [Javellus.] [Abulens.] [Bannez.] [Nazar.] [Gonzalez] [Ripa.] [Petrus a Jesu.] **44** Which the Angelic Doctor also taught on book 4 of the Sentences, dist. 12, q. 1, a. 1, quæstiuncula 3 ad 3, and in III, q. 77, a. 2, where he requires two things for the individuation of a material thing, namely the subject, or matter, and quantity. And finally he maintains this same doctrine in I, q. 3, a. 2 ad 3; q. 30, a. 3; q. 50, a. 4; q. 54, a. 3 ad 2; q. 56, a. 1 ad 2; qq. 75 and 85, a. 1 ad 2; and Contra Gentiles I, chap. 21, and Contra Gentiles II, chap. 92, and in the question De malo, a. 1 ad 8, and very often elsewhere. Whence both Aristotle and the holy Doctor are followed by Capreolus, on book 2, dist. 3, q. 1, a. 2, concl. 2, and a. 3, in the solution to the arguments of Scotus adduced in the second place against the same conclusion; Cajetan, De ente et essentia, chap. 2, q. 5; Soncinas, on Metaphysics VII, qq. 33 and 34; Ferrara, on Contra Gentiles I, chap. 21; Flandria, on Metaphysics VII, q. 22, a. 3; Javellus, on Metaphysics V, q. 15; the Abulensis, tome 2 on chap. 23; of Exodus, q. 60, about the middle; Bannez, on I, q. 3, a. 2 ad 2, and on 1 De generatione, chap. 5, q. 3, a. 8, asserting that our assertion, as it stands, is to be accepted by all who profess themselves disciples of St. Thomas. The same is maintained also by Nazarius, on I, q. 3, a. 2, sole controversy; Gonzalez, q. 11, a. 1 ad 2, disp. 23, § 2, concl. 9; Ripa, q. 3, a. 2; Petrus a Jesu Maria, otherwise Serna of Seville, in his Logic, disp. 3, sect. 5, q. 3, a. 1, asserting that this is the undoubted opinion of Aristotle and St. Thomas; and finally very many others.

[The response of Suarez.] **45** Nor does it matter if with Suarez you reply that Aristotle does not seem ever to have investigated and declared the principle of individuation ex professo and metaphysically, but only, from sensible things, after the manner of the physicist and as it were from a sign, to have taught that one material individual is distinguished in number from another through quantity and the other sensible accidents, or through matter signed by quantity. And thus, [on this reply,] the aforesaid matter has the character of the aforesaid principle only as to *being known* (cognosci) and *being sensed* (sentiri), not as to *esse*. In which way also, he says, St. Thomas is to be understood when he so often assigns matter itself signed by quantity as the first principle of individuation of material substance.

[It is rejected] This, then, does not matter. First, because it would be strange that Aristotle should have treated at once, and in the same text, both of analogous, generic, and specific unity and of numerical unity, and that to those former he should have assigned their proper principle and root as to *esse*, but to numerical unity alone should have given a principle as to *being known*, and not as to *esse*. Again: because Aristotle in the Metaphysics, and in metaphysical fashion, treated of unity as such, from the fact that it stands after the manner of a passion (passio) with respect to being, which is the proper object of Metaphysics. And further, because the ratio of unity as such becomes greatly known from the fact that those diverse modes of unities are known, namely the analogous, the generic, and the specific, he therefore treated of them in the same Metaphysics and in metaphysical fashion, assigning their proper principle as to their *esse*: therefore, when in the same place he speaks at the same time of numerical unity, assigning its principle, he also treated of it, and of its principle, as to *esse*, and in metaphysical fashion: since the cognition of this unity, as to its *esse* and as to its root, conduces no less to an exact knowledge of unity than does the cognition of the other unities. Above all because, as we saw from the same Aristotle in the preceding dubium, the proper principle, as to *esse*, of the aforesaid unity cannot be the form, but matter, as determined to this individual, or substantial composite — which, as will be established from what is presently to be said, is nothing other than matter signed by quantity in some one of the aforesaid ways.

**46** From this it also follows that this exposition of Father Suarez cannot be adapted to the testimonies of St. Thomas. First, because Aristotle and the Angelic Doctor speak in the same way. Again: because the reasons (rationes) which we adduced in the preceding dubium from Aristotle and St. Thomas either prove nothing, or they persuade that matter signed by quantity is the principle of individuation as to *esse*, and not only as to *being known*, as will be established to anyone who inspects them. Further, because the holy Teacher, both in the place cited from III, q. 77, a. 2, and in opusculum 70, q. 4, a. 2, and often elsewhere, gathers thence that matter, not taken in just any way, but signed by quantity, is the first principle of individuation of the whole substantial composite: because quantity of itself, as from a first root, in a certain way has a certain individuation; and therefore rightly must matter, as it stands under quantity (in the manner to be stated at no. 116 and following), be the first principle of individuation, according to that common maxim, namely: nothing is such unless it be such of itself, or in some way stand under that which of itself is such. Now this reason (ratio) — as is evident of itself, and will be more fully established from what is to be said at nos. 49, 91, and 92, and in the following disputation from no. 21 up to no. 27, as also at no. 38 — proves that matter as it stands under quantity, or signed by quantity, has the character of the first principle of individuation not only as to *being known*, but also as to *esse*.

Finally: because this exposition of Father Suarez openly contradicts several testimonies of St. Thomas which we adduced at nos. 9 and 10, as will be established to one who examines them. But especially the testimony of the same holy Doctor, opusc. 70, q. 4, a. 2, where he has as follows: *Matter considered according to itself is the principle of diversity neither according to species nor according to number: but just as it is the principle of diversity according to genus insofar as it stands under a common form, so it is the principle of diversity according to number insofar as it stands under indeterminate dimensions (dimensiones interminatæ): and therefore, since these dimensions are of the genus of accidents, diversity according to number is sometimes reduced to the diversity of matter, sometimes to the diversity of accidents, and this by reason of the aforesaid dimensions. The other accidents, however, are not the principle of individuation, but they are the principle of knowing the distinction of individuals; and in this way individuation is attributed also to the other accidents.*

**47** But if matter signed by quantity, and hence quantity, were not in a certain way, and in the sense to be explained below at no. 116 and following, the principle of individuation of material substance as to *esse*, then that difference would be null which St. Thomas, in the place just adduced, establishes between quantity and the other accidents: since the remaining accidents too are an a posteriori sign of the individuation of the substantial composite, and it is rendered demonstrable through them.

[St. Thomas.] [Suarez.] Whence, against this, those two testimonies from St. Thomas — opusc. 32, De natura materiæ et dimensionibus interminatis, chap. 3, and opusc. 29, De principio individuationis — which Suarez, in disp. 5 of his Metaphysics, sect. 3, adduces in his own favor, do not press; as will be established from the things we shall adduce below at no. 75: nay rather, they greatly favor us, as will appear more fully from the things that will be said at no. 139.
