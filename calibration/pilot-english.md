*[The Prooemium of St. Thomas to q. 14 concludes:]* . . . but the exemplar-notions (rationes) of things, according as they are in God who knows them, are called ideas; hence to the consideration of knowledge there will also have to be joined a consideration of the ideas. Therefore, so that everything the holy Doctor hands down in this question may stand fully explained, it has seemed good to examine this brief yet most profound Prooemium of St. Thomas, in which the rationale of the order of this question with respect to the preceding ones is contained.

## SOLE DUBIUM.

*Whether the things that St. Thomas teaches in the Prooemium of this question are true?*

This dubium is to be dispatched briefly, so that we may pass on to the other, loftier matters that arise in this treatise.

### § I. Reasons for doubting.

[First.] The first reason for doubting is that what he premises in the first place appears false — namely, that he is here treating of the things that pertain to the *operation* of God, after the consideration of those that regard the divine *substance*. For the divine act of understanding regards the substance and essence of God — not in just any manner, but as his proper and highest constitutive, as we shall say at greater length at article 4 of this question; and hence the disputation concerning it contained in this question will belong to the treatise and disputation on the things that pertain to the substance of God.

There is the further point that the holy Doctor, after this question on God's knowledge, disputes concerning the truth and the life of God; yet it is evident that truth does not import an operation, but a conformity and consonance with the thing, and likewise that life pertains to the very substance of the living thing. Consequently St. Thomas in these questions treats of many things regarding the substance of God, insofar as, according to our manner of understanding, it is distinguished from operation; and so he seems not to have spoken rightly in saying that with this fourteenth question he begins the disputation on what pertains to the operation of God, the matters regarding his substance being already finished.

**2** [Second.] The second reason for doubting is drawn from what the holy Doctor said in the place cited, namely: since operation is twofold — the one immanent, the other transient — one must treat first of knowledge and will, because to understand is in the one understanding and to will is in the one willing, and afterwards of power, which is considered as the principle of exterior effects. For, first of all, the holy Doctor does not seem to have rightly compared these two extremes with each other. Granted that, if the comparison is made between immanent and transient operation, the former is prior and the latter posterior — which is why the same St. Thomas, after the treatise on the divine processions and the other things necessarily required for them, treated of the operation *ad extra* — nevertheless, if the comparison is made between immanent operation and the *power* of God, then the immanent operation is not prior to the divine power but the reverse: just as, universally, the powers of any nature whatsoever are prior to its immanent operations. Therefore, etc.

**3** [Third.] Next: God does not act *ad extra* through a formally transient operation, but through the act of the divine command (imperium), according to the text: *He spoke, and they were made; he commanded, and they were created.* Therefore St. Thomas wrongly drew the distinction ordering these questions — namely, that in some of them the immanent actions of God are treated, while in others the power, considered as the principle of the divine operation proceeding into an exterior effect, and hence as the principle of a transient action.

Nor will it suffice to reply that, although the act of command is formally immanent — being indeed an act of the intellect, as St. Thomas teaches in I-II, q. 17, a. 1 — it is nevertheless *virtually* transient, and that he could therefore dispute of it in different questions, considered under these different aspects.

For against this stands the fact that, in order to treat of the immanent divine acts as virtually transient, there was no need to institute distinct questions. And indeed, in this very question, and in question 19 on the will of God, his knowledge and will are treated precisely as they are causes of things, and hence precisely as virtually transient.

### § II. Decision of the dubium.

**4** [Assertion.] It must nevertheless be said that what St. Thomas taught in this prooemium is true, and hence that he observed the best method and the most beautiful order in handing down these questions. So say his disciples commonly, and a great many others besides. The first part of the assertion will be sufficiently evident from what is to be said below and from the solutions of the reasons for doubting. The second part follows from the first, since what is principally contained in St. Thomas's prooemium is precisely this order and method. Moreover, this same part is proved from no better source than the rationale of order that he himself adduces at the opening of the question, which can be set out more fully as follows.

For in the first place he treated of the divine *esse* and of the other — so to speak — *entitative* attributes, which pertain to the substance of God after the manner of a quasi-property: such are infinity, immutability, eternity, and invisibility to the created intellect by its own powers; and finally, in the immediately preceding question, he explained how all these things ought to be expressed and signified by words and names. Since, then, the entitative attributes (to speak thus) are prior to the operative attributes, or divine operations, as is self-evident, it follows that they were rightly treated first; and since among operations the immanent are prior to the transient — for the latter are directed by the former to their end — and among the immanent, cognition, or knowledge, is prior to volition, he therefore with the best right discussed God's knowledge in the first place after the entitative attributes.

### § III. Solution of the reasons for doubting adduced at the outset.

**5** [To the first.] Hence, to what we objected in the first place against the prooemium of this question, it must be said that although the act of understanding (taken in the sense to be explained below at article 4) pertains to the intrinsic concept of God as his differential ratio and ultimate constitutive, nevertheless the title of this question does not concern it under that aspect, but under the *formality of knowledge* (sub formalitate scientiæ); and under this aspect it does not pertain, according to our manner of understanding, to the intrinsic constitutive of God, as we shall say in this same treatise, disputation 1, no. 68.

To what is adduced in the same place concerning the questions on the truth and the life of God, it must be said that St. Thomas treated of truth within these questions on the operative attributes because, as he himself notes in the prooemium of this question, *knowledge is of true things*; and therefore he had to treat of truth and falsity. Likewise, because *to understand and to know is a certain kind of living*, therefore, as the Angelic Doctor says in the same place, after the consideration of the divine knowledge, the divine life must be considered.

Nor is the further point there raised of any weight — namely, that life pertains to the concept of the substance and essence of God, just as in us too life regards the quidditative concept of our substance and essence; so that the holy Doctor ought rather to have treated of the life of God in a question preceding this one on God's knowledge than in the questions following it.

For the reply is that life can pertain both to the concept of the essence and to the concept of the vital powers and actions, as is evident from what St. Thomas teaches in question 18 of this First Part, and from what our Complutenses assert in book 1 of their *De Anima*. And therefore the holy Doctor could treat of the life of God in its own right after this question on God's knowledge. And the reason why he treated more particularly and expressly of the life of God's vital operations, flowing as it were from his essence, is this: that for us the notion of life is known above all, among all other things, through vital operation — that is, through something's being moved by itself. So true is this that even things which merely seem to move of themselves, though in truth they are not so moved, are commonly stamped by us with the name of life: as quicksilver (*argentum vivum*, "living silver"), living water, and the like.

[Rejoinder.] Nor again is it of any weight, should you press the point, that even speaking of the life of vital operations not pertaining to the essence, the holy Doctor would seem to have been obliged to treat of the life of God before treating of his knowledge, since that notion of life is more common and more universal, and hence prior, than the notion of knowledge.

[Solution.] For the reply is that a science that treats of some species as its adequate object need not treat first of the more remote and more universal — as is the case in Theology, whose adequate object is God himself.

**6** [To the second.] To the second it is replied that although in creatures the immanent operations of intellect and will are not prior to their operative powers, but rather posterior, nevertheless in God they are in a certain way prior on the side of our manner of conceiving. For the immanent acts of his intellect and will, insofar as they are virtually transient and have efficacy to produce whatever shall have pleased him, take on the character of *omnipotence*; and since in these acts, according to our manner of understanding, their being immanent acts of understanding and willing is prior to their having the aforesaid efficacy and virtual power of acting transiently, as we are just now explaining, therefore in this question, and in question 19 of this First Part, they are treated first under the former consideration rather than under this latter one.

[To the third.] From this the answer to the third reason for doubting is clear. For we say that the solution there proposed is legitimate. And to the proof [it must be said] that, although treating of the immanent acts of the divine intellect and will as virtually transient *after the manner of what directs and intends* future effects does pertain to this question — since this is proper to intellect and will as distinguished from the executing power — nevertheless, treating of these same acts as identical with omnipotence, in the way to be stated below, and hence as virtually transient *after the manner of what executes* in time that which from eternity is directed by the divine knowledge and is intended and prefined (præfinitum) by the will of God, belongs not undeservedly to a distinct question: since, as we shall see below, God exercises his influence through these acts in one way under the former consideration, and in another way through the same acts under the latter.
